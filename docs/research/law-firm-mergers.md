# Large law firm mergers, 2005-2025

Research dossier for game balance. Compiled 2026-11-25 from public web sources.

## Introduction

This file collects real-world benchmarks on law firm combinations: how many happen each year, how the big ones were structured, what they cost in partners and clients, why some failed, and how the Swiss verein works. It ends with proposed game rules, including target band T13 (partner loss within 24 months of a merger).

Conventions:

- Tags: **S** = the figure appears in the cited source; **D** = derived from sourced figures (the arithmetic is shown); **A** = an assumption or estimate (the basis is stated). "not found" means searched for without success; the note says what was searched.
- Facts are firm-level only. Individuals are not named, and no conduct is attributed to any person.
- Most trade sources (The American Lawyer, Law.com International, Legal Business, Law360, Bloomberg Law) are paywalled. Where a figure was seen only in a search-engine excerpt or the free opening paragraphs of a paywalled page, the note says "(excerpt)".
- The two US trackers count differently. Altman Weil MergerLine (2007-2020) counts deals when **announced** and includes acquired firms of 2+ lawyers. Fairfax Associates counts deals when **completed** and only where the acquired firm has 5+ lawyers [14]. Their totals are not comparable: in 2019 Altman Weil counted 115 and Fairfax 59 [11][18].

Points ratios: no public source gives the points or profit-share ratio set in any of these deals (searched "points ratio", "profit share" with each firm name, and "merger profit gap rule of thumb"). One consultant said "there's no rule of thumb" on how profit per partner should move after a merger (S, excerpt [37b]). Bloomberg Law's analysis of the 18 largest mergers in 15 years found about two-thirds grew PEP and revenue per lawyer more slowly than competitors afterwards, and all but three trailed average Am Law 100 revenue growth S [37].

### Names

- Typical pattern: combine one lead name from each side (Hogan Lovells, Norton Rose Fulbright, Herbert Smith Freehills, K&L Gates, Squire Patton Boggs, A&O Shearman, HSF Kramer). The larger or lead firm's name comes first in every listed case (A, from section 2).
- Dentons dropped "SNR" and added an "S" S [25b]. CMS kept its group brand; the UK partnership became CMS Cameron McKenna Nabarro Olswang LLP S [34]. KWM's SJ Berwin unit traded temporarily as "King & Wood Mallesons SJ Berwin" S [33].

### Governance

- Joint leadership is common in mergers of near-equals: Hogan Lovells had co-CEOs and co-chairs, from each legacy firm (S [22]); BCLP had co-chairs from each firm S [35].
- Takeovers keep one leader: Norton Rose's group chief executive led the combined firm S [24]. Squire Sanders' 12-member executive committee was expanded to add Patton Boggs members S [28].
- Hogan Lovells' co-CEO arrangement ran until 2014, about four years S [22].

### Compensation harmonisation

- Lovells dropped its flat 2:1 lockstep and largely adopted Hogan's contribution-based system S [23b].
- HSF kept two pay systems at go-live and planned a later vote on one S [27].
- Fulbright's London partners were kept out of Norton Rose's UK remuneration system at the start (S, excerpt [24e]).
- A&O Shearman moved to an all-equity partnership with a three-tier modified lockstep in October 2024, five months after go-live S [30d].
- Norton Rose Fulbright changed its rules in March 2023, ten years after the merger, to give US partners credit for cross-border work they originated S [24d].
- A (game): pay harmonisation takes 0-24 months for full mergers and may never happen in a verein.

### Rate harmonisation

- "Are billing rates going to increase?" is "almost universally the first question clients ask", especially when a smaller firm joins a larger one (S, excerpt [43]).
- Mitigations consultants recommend: a clear public position on rates, holding a partner's rate, or moving to a blended rate (S, excerpt [43]).
- Critics of K&L Gates' national expansion said it pushed rates up and created conflicts (S, excerpt [37]).
- No public figure for a merged firm's rate change was found.

### Proposed T13 band

- **T13: 10-20% of the combined partnership is gone within 24 months of go-live, with a central value of ~14%.** This counts every exit: voluntary leavers, retirements and managed de-equitisations. A&O Shearman's 17% in 17 months, including its 10% planned cut, sits near the top. Hogan Lovells' ~4% before go-live and Pirical's 4% average sit at the bottom of the window, and 24 months of normal turnover adds 8-12% (A, above).
- **Tail:** 30% or more only when one side is already distressed (Patton Boggs, KWM Europe, Dewey & LeBoeuf). The game should reach that range only through a distress flag, not through merger randomness.
- **Excess over baseline:** a merger adds about 3-8 percentage points of partner loss over 24 months. This is a judgement: Pirical's 4% merger-window spike (S) plus 0-4 points of post-close attrition (A).

## 5. Client losses from conflicts

- Orrick + Pillsbury (2013): talks called off over "irresolvable client conflicts" between Orrick's public finance clients and Pillsbury's tax, environmental and real estate practices, before any letter of intent or vote. The combined firm would have had ~$1.4bn revenue (~1,000 + 600+ lawyers) S [40].
- Squire Sanders + Patton Boggs (2014): the deal was conditioned on resolving a major litigation matter involving Patton Boggs S [28].
- Troutman Pepper + Locke Lord (2024): some Locke Lord lawyers expected to be unable to represent longtime clients; the firm reportedly said new Troutman work would make up for it S [41]. A few lawyers left over the conflicts S [41b].
- Hogan Lovells + Cadwalader (2026, outside scope): two Cadwalader litigation leaders left after a conflicts review against Hogan Lovells' clients S [42].
- Redgrave + Nelson Mullins' e-discovery unit (2021): called off at the last minute over "conflict-related issues" (S, excerpt [42b]).
- Waivers: firms ask clients for conflict waivers, but clients often decline S [41].
- Client sentiment: Acritas (Sharplegal 2015) found "more than a fifth" (21%) of clients less likely to use a firm after it merges S [39]. That is intent, not measured loss.
- Typical share of revenue lost to conflicts: not found (searched "merger conflicts percentage of revenue", "clients dropped merger conflicts consultant estimate").

## 6. Rate harmonisation and low-rate clients

- When a larger firm enters a market by taking over a local firm, higher rates "often cause the small firm's long-term clients to seek more affordable counsel" (S, excerpt [43b]).
- Mergers are easier when rates are already close; Womble Bond Dickinson + Lewis Roca was cited as a case of similar rate and pay structures (S, excerpt [43c]).
- Locke Lord's gross revenue fell nearly 17% in the years after its 2015 tie-up with Edwards Wildman S [37]. The source does not attribute the fall to rates or clients.
- No quantified loss of low-rate clients after a merger was found (searched "rate increase clients leave after merger", "rate harmonization clients", "insurance clients rates merger").

## 8. The Swiss verein

What it is:

- A verein is a Swiss association, similar to an Anglo-American voluntary association. The verein does not practise law. Member firms keep their own legal identity, profit pool and liability, and share a brand, strategy, IT and central management S [50][50e].
- Costs: members pay into a central verein fund for shared management overheads S [50]. NRF shares costs but not profits between profit centres S [24]. The cost-allocation formula: not found in any public source (searched "verein cost sharing formula").
- Profits: separate profit pools per member (Baker McKenzie: regional profit pools with their own tax, accounting and partner pay systems S [50e]; Hogan Lovells: two profit pools S [23]; NRF: five profit pools when reported, S excerpt [24d]).

| Firm | Verein | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Baker McKenzie | from 2004, the first major law firm verein S [50e] | the only verein formed from one existing partnership S [50e] |
| DLA Piper | yes S [50] | partners voted in April 2026 to dissolve it for a global holding company over two partnerships; reports differ on how far profits merge S [50f] |
| Hogan Lovells | yes, from 2010 S [23] | separate legal entities (Hogan Lovells International LLP, Hogan Lovells US LLP) S [50g] |
| Norton Rose Fulbright | yes S [24] | US, UK, Canada and South Africa entities are members S [24c] |
| Dentons | yes, from 2013 S [25] | Dacheng joined in 2015 and left in 2023 S [26] |
| Squire Patton Boggs | yes (Squire Sanders) S [50] | - |
| King & Wood Mallesons | yes, from 2012 S [33] | legacy SJ Berwin was the fourth member S [33] |

Liability and criticism:

- Selling point: liability is ring-fenced between members. Attempts to sue a verein for another member's acts had failed by 2016 (the Parmalat case) S [50].
- Conflicts: US bar regulators may treat all offices as one firm. A US ITC judge disqualified Dentons US in 2015 because the shared brand and systems undercut its claim to be separate S [50]. In 2020 an Ohio jury awarded $32.3m against Dentons US over a conflict arising from its Canadian member's work (S [50b]); the appeal was lost in 2022 S [50c].
- Liability: in 2024 an Illinois appellate court kept a malpractice case against Baker McKenzie in the US forum, citing alleged "legal unity of liability" S [50b]. The questions remain open in the academic literature S [50d].
- Performance: The Economist (2013-14) found vereins underperform peers (as cited S [50]). Cross-referral fees can look like fee-sharing under ABA Model Rule 1.5(e), and data sharing between members is hard S [50].
- Integration risk: KWM Europe (legacy SJ Berwin) saw profitability fall four months after joining. It cut 15% of its partners, saw a planned £14m partner recapitalisation collapse, and won only 16% support for a bail-out plan before the European arm failed in 2016-17 S [33b][33c].
- Trend: DLA Piper (2026) and NRF (financial year moved to the US calendar) are moving towards integration S [50f][24d]. Full mergers with one profit pool (HSF 2012, HSF Kramer 2025, A&O Shearman 2024) are the alternative S [27][31][30].

## Implications for the game

Proposals for the PT merger system. Each is tagged.

1. **Deal frequency** (A, from section 1): a US market of ~1,000 firms produces 40-115 combinations a year; about 80% acquire a firm of 20 or fewer lawyers, and only 1-5 a year join two 100+ lawyer firms. Rival AI should make one transformational merger every few years, not every year.
2. **Points-ratio rule of thumb** (A): give the lower-PEP firm's partners points in proportion to the profit each side brings: share = legacy profit pool / combined profit pool, with a per-partner discount equal to the PEP gap. Below a 10% PEP gap (Ashurst/Blakes at ~90% was the threshold for full merger, S [32]), allow a single pool from day one. At a 10-30% gap, use a single pool with a 12-24 month transition, or a verein. Above 30%, the higher-PEP side's vote fails unless synergies cover the gap. The 10% point is anchored on Ashurst; the 30% cut-off is a judgement (no source).
3. **T13, partner loss within 24 months of go-live** (A, section 4): **10-20%** of the combined partnership, central ~14%, counting all exits. The excess over a no-merger baseline is +3 to +8 points. Losses fall mostly on the smaller or lower-PEP side and on overlapping offices. Reach 30%+ only when a party is distressed.
4. **Clients lost to conflicts** (A, section 5): 1-5% of combined revenue for a typical merger of large firms; the deal dies before the vote when conflicts exceed ~10% of either firm's revenue (Orrick/Pillsbury). No source gives a percentage; the basis is that conflicts kill some deals outright (S [40]), while firms that go ahead accept "some" conflicts (S [42]). A sentiment hit: about 1 in 5 clients is "less likely" to use the firm (S [39]), modelled as a small retention penalty in year 1, not as an outright loss.
5. **Rate harmonisation client loss** (A, section 6): when the lower-rate firm's rates rise by more than ~10%, it loses 3-10% of that firm's revenue from rate-sensitive clients (insurers, mid-market, public sector) within 12 months; there is no loss if rates are held or blended. No source gives a figure; the basis is the consultant observation that rate rises push small-firm clients to cheaper counsel (S [43b]).
6. **Diligence length and cost** (S/A): talks to completion take 4-9 months for a domestic deal (S, excerpt [51]) and 12-18+ months for a transatlantic one (A&O/O'Melveny ran ~18 months, S [44]). Financial diligence takes 30-90 days (S, excerpt [51b]). No source gives the cost (not found). A (game): 0.5-1.5% of the smaller firm's annual revenue in advisers and partner time.
7. **Vote thresholds** (S, section 7): 75% for UK-style partnerships and two-thirds for US ones, optionally weighted by equity points. Votes that reach the floor usually pass with 90%+. Model failure mainly as a pre-vote collapse (conflicts, pay, culture), at about 30-40% of announced talks among large firms (A: of the large talks in sections 2 and 7, 8 failures against 17 completions = 32% D; the sample over-represents publicised talks).
8. **Verein option** (S/A, section 8): it keeps separate profit pools and liability and shares brand costs by formula (the formula is a game design choice, A: by revenue share). It lowers the vote hurdle and partner loss but caps cross-referral synergy, and it carries a conflicts-imputation risk (Dentons, S [50]). Allow a later "full integration" vote.
