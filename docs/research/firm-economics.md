# US law firm economics benchmarks (2005, 2009, 2015)

Research dossier for game balance. Compiled 2026-11-23 from public web sources.

## Introduction

This file collects real-world benchmarks for US law firm economics in three years (2005, 2009, 2015) and three tiers: the Am Law 100, the Am Law Second Hundred (ranks 101-200), and mid-size firms of roughly 50-150 lawyers. It also covers how fast some real young firms grew, and associate attrition.

Conventions:

- Am Law rankings published in year N report fiscal year N-1. "2005" below means fiscal 2005 (the 2006 Am Law 100), and so on.
- Am Law definitions: RPL = gross revenue / lawyers (FTE); PEP = net operating income / equity partners ([5]). Am Law "averages" are aggregates (sum over sum), not means of firms.
- Tags: **S** = the figure appears in the cited source; **D** = derived from sourced figures (the arithmetic is shown); **A** = an assumption or estimate (the basis is stated). "not found" means searched for without success; the note says what was searched.
- Many primary sources (American Lawyer, NALP bulletins, Citi Hildebrandt advisories, Wells Fargo LSG) are paywalled. Where a figure was seen only in a search-engine excerpt of a paywalled page, the note says so.
- Mid-size firm data is thin. Public surveys either cover all firm sizes or the Am Law 200; there is no public 50-150 lawyer aggregate for RPL or PEP.

## 3. Profit margin (net operating income / revenue)

| Tier | 2005 | 2009 | 2015 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Am Law 100 | not found | 36.6% D [13] | 39.5% D [14] |
| Second Hundred | not found | 36% S [6] | not found; A ~35% [15] |
| Mid-size (50-150) | not found | not found | not found; A 28-35% [16] |

13. PEP × equity partners / revenue = $1.26M × 18,808 / $64.8bn = 36.6% [2].
14. Profits per lawyer / RPL = $352,898 / $894,253 = 39.5% [3][17]. Context: the Am Law 100 margin was 40.14% in 1987 and 40.03% in 2018, and margins are cyclical (they fall in recessions) [18]. Peer Monitor shows all-segment margins "stagnant to declining" over 2007-2016, on a chart scaled 30-46% (values not legible in the PDF text) [19].
15. A: carried forward from the 2009 figure of 36%. With Second Hundred PEP and RPL both flat in 2015, the margin was probably flat too.
16. A: Altman Weil's 2002 Survey of Law Firm Economics (all sizes) shows lawyer compensation at 57.3% of gross receipts and everything else at 42.7% [20]. Partner income is part of that 57.3%, so the "net income" margin of a mid-size firm is lower than the Am Law tiers. 28-35% is a judgement, not a sourced figure.

Notes: shares = band median / scale (e.g. 2005: 83,000 / 125,000 = 0.664). NALP warns that the respondent pool changes each year; in 2015 only about 5% of respondents were firms of 50 or fewer lawyers, so the ≤50 figure is unreliable [29]. In 2009 the median eighth-year associate ranged from $111,625 (small firms) to $258,000 (largest) [28]. **Benchmark: mid-size firms pay about two-thirds to three-quarters of the big-firm first-year scale.**

Takeaways for the game (A): an elite start-up can grow 15-25% a year for its first 15-20 years, starting from 3-10 lawyers. Boies Schiller is the outlier: it reached ~170 in six years through star lateral hires. Mature elite firms slow to 5-8% a year. The Am Law 200 average was 2.6% equity-partner growth in 2007 (21-year average 3.2%) [9]. Kirkland's early growth: not found (not searched in depth).

## 9. Attrition, partnership odds, firm size

- **Associate attrition (NALP Foundation, Update on Associate Attrition):** 2005, 2009 and 2015 annual rates not found (the reports are sold, not public). Nearest figures: an average annual rate of 19% (S, Foley citing the NALP Foundation, undated [46]); 2020: 20%; 2021: 26% (series high); 2023: 18%; 2024: 20%; 2025: 19% (S [47]). In 2000, about 59% of associates had left within about five years; Hildebrandt put the figure near 80% by 2005 (S [48]). Firms lost 15 associates for every 20 hired over the period (S [49]). 83% of 2025 leavers left within five years of hire (S [47]).
- **Share of a class making partner:** not found. Searched NALP, the NALP Foundation and Galanter & Henderson's "Elastic Tournament". A (derived from the above): with ~75-80% gone within five or six years and EPs at ~22% of Am Law 100 lawyers, roughly 5-15% of a big-firm entering class becomes an equity partner at that firm. This is a judgement, not a sourced figure.
- **Am Law 200 median headcount, 2015:** not found. Averages (D): Am Law 100, 92,983 / 100 ≈ 930 lawyers [3][17]; Second Hundred, $18.7bn / $649,023 = 28,812, so ≈ 288 lawyers (Bloomberg gives 28,937 for 2015 [17]). The average Second Hundred firm had 289 lawyers in 2007 (S [4]). The NLJ 350 average was 421 lawyers (S, around 2015 [50]).
