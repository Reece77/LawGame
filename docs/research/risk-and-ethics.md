# Risk and ethics benchmarks: malpractice, insurance, discipline, trust accounts, billing disputes

Research dossier for game balance. Compiled 2026-11-25 from public web sources.

## Introduction

This file collects real-world benchmarks for the risk side of running a law firm: how often firms are sued for malpractice and what claims cost, what professional liability insurance costs and how it is structured, how often lawyers are disciplined and for what, the trust-account (client money) rules in the US and England and Wales, and how clients push back on bills (e-billing review and fee arbitration). The reference firm is a US (New York) commercial firm growing from 4 lawyers in 2005 to 50-250 lawyers by 2015, possibly with a London office; balance is read over 2005-2015.

Conventions:

- Tags: **S** = the figure appears in the cited source; **D** = derived from sourced figures (the arithmetic is shown); **A** = an assumption or estimate (the basis is stated). "not found" means searched for without success; the note says what was searched.
- ADR PT-0013: aggregate statistics only. No real individual lawyer is named in connection with any claim, sanction or misconduct, and no firm is named in connection with a specific claim or sanction. Institutions (ALAS, the SRA, the ABA, insurers as survey participants) are named only for aggregate facts.
- Many primary sources are paywalled or blocked to automated fetches: the ABA Profile of Legal Malpractice Claims (sold by the ABA), the ABA Survey on Lawyer Discipline Systems (S.O.L.D.) PDFs (americanbar.org returned HTTP 403), ALAS annual reports (not public). Where a figure was seen only in a search-engine excerpt, the note says so. The best single public source on large-firm claims is Baker and Swedloff (2015) [1], which compiles ALAS annual reports 1983-2013 and the ABA Profiles to 2008-2011; its figures were read from the full PDF.
- The ABA Profile is drawn mostly from insurers of solo and small firms. It describes the shape of claims (practice area, error type, disposition), not large-firm frequency ([1] p. 1300).

1. A: no source measures errors that escape review. Basis: the game needs most escaped errors to be harmless or unnoticed by the client. With about 3 notices per 100 lawyers a year, a 15% conversion implies about 20 material escaped errors per 100 lawyers a year, roughly one per five lawyers. That is plausible against a 2015 finding that 21% of lawyers rounded up their time [41], and it keeps claims rare. Tune this number first if claim frequency misses the band.
